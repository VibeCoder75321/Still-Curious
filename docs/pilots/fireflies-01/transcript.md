# How do fireflies make light?

Accepted text pilot · 15 October 2026 · 600 words · 9 turns

Adela Wren hosts three fictional specialists. Audio has not been generated.

**Adela Wren**

How does a firefly make its own light—not just reflect light from somewhere else? Our three fictional specialists are Mira Venn, an enzyme biochemist; Tomas Rell, who studies insect cells; and Leena Sorel, whose field is molecular light emission. Tomas, let’s begin inside the insect: what are the light-producing cells, and how do they give us a starting point?

**Tomas Rell**

The light-producing cells are called photocytes, and they form part of a specialised tissue—the light organ. In the adult firefly Aquatica leii, for example, the organ has a distinct light-producing layer containing these cells.

There’s also a useful link to the chemistry. A separate microscopy study used antibody-based labels to locate luciferase—the enzyme involved in light production—in tiny granules within photocytes.

That tells us where the enzyme is found. It doesn’t yet explain how a chemical reaction produces light.

**Adela Wren**

Mira, we’ve located the enzyme in those cells. What does it help happen? Walk us from the reacting molecule to light, keeping the jobs of luciferin, luciferase, ATP and oxygen distinct.

**Mira Venn**

Start with luciferin: that’s the molecule being chemically changed. Luciferase—the similar-sounding name—is an enzyme, a catalyst that helps those changes happen.

In the pathway described by biochemical reviews, ATP first helps activate luciferin. ATP is a molecule cells use to power chemical work; here its role is to prepare luciferin for the next reaction. Activation isn’t yet the light.

The activated luciferin then reacts with oxygen. That chemistry produces a different molecule, oxyluciferin, in an excited state—meaning it has extra energy. When it returns to a lower-energy state, it releases a photon, a small packet of light.

So luciferase enables the reaction; it isn’t itself a little glowing bulb.

**Adela Wren**

Leena, let’s pause at that last step. When we say a molecule is ‘excited’ and then releases a packet of light, what is actually changing? Can you give us a physical picture without equations?

**Leena Sorel**

“Excited” here means a higher-energy electronic state—a change in the energy arrangement of the molecule’s electrons. It doesn’t simply mean the molecule is hotter or moving faster.

Picture two steps, representing energy rather than physical height. The chemistry leaves oxyluciferin on the upper step. When it makes the transition to the lower one, it can release the energy difference as a photon—a packet of light that travels away.

That describes the light-emitting route, though, not a guarantee that every reaction event makes light. The reviewed pathway also includes a branch that produces none.

**Adela Wren**

Mira, you’ve described the pathway from biochemical reviews. What would a controlled experiment need to show to test those molecular roles—and why isn’t finding luciferase inside a cell enough?

**Mira Venn**

A possible test would use a defined reaction mixture containing luciferin, luciferase, ATP and oxygen, under suitable controlled conditions. First establish that it produces measurable light; then omit one component at a time, keeping everything else matched.

If omitting ATP stopped the light, and adding it back restored it, that would support ATP being required under those conditions. It wouldn’t yet prove the activation step. To distinguish the steps, you’d need chemical measurements of intermediates and products as well as light.

That’s a hypothetical design, not a reported experiment.

The labels show where luciferase is detected—not whether it’s active or how the reaction proceeds.

**Adela Wren**

So the firefly makes light through chemistry in specialised cells. ATP helps activate luciferin; reaction with oxygen produces excited oxyluciferin, which releases light as its energy falls. Luciferase is the catalyst, not the glowing molecule. We’ve distinguished that review-supported pathway from microscopy locating the enzyme—not a demonstration of every chemical step. Mira, Tomas and Leena, thank you. And thank you for listening.
