Productivity asks how much usable product the equipment can deliver over time. Waste asks how much material is discarded in obtaining that product. Neither is the same as yield, which relates the product obtained to what the starting material could theoretically supply.

Imagine, hypothetically, two processes with the same yield. One occupies the equipment for less time, so it can deliver more product over a month. That’s a productivity advantage without a yield advantage. If it also discards less material for each kilogram of acceptable product, that’s a separate waste advantage.

To weigh the reported gains together, I’d put both processes on that common basis: the same amount of product meeting the same quality requirements. Then I’d ask where the accounting starts and stops. Does the equipment time include separation, or only the reaction? Does the waste figure cover the same stretch of the process? The phrase ‘with existing equipment’ is encouraging because it connects the reported productivity gain to equipment already available. But it doesn’t tell us that operating conditions stayed unchanged, or precisely what time was counted.

I wouldn’t add those figures into a single score—or assume they arose independently. One process change could contribute to both.
