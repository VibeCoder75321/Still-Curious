# Making the Same Drug a Different Way

**Nell Arden**

Welcome to Still Curious. Making a drug molecule once is an achievement. Making it by a route that works for manufacturing is a different challenge. What makes one way of producing the same medicine worth replacing with another?

Our case is sitagliptin, a diabetes medicine. We’re looking at making its active ingredient, not the finished tablet. Joining me are three fictional specialists: Mira Calder in process chemistry, Tomas Venn in biocatalysis, and Leena Orrell in process engineering.

The surprising starting point is an enzyme—a biological catalyst that helps a reaction happen—which initially showed no detectable activity on the target molecule.

Tomas, what foothold did slight activity on a smaller, related molecule give the developers?

**Tomas Venn**

It gave them a small, measurable sign of a related ability—an enzyme worth trying to change.

The available enzymes hadn’t shown detectable activity on the sitagliptin starting material. But one did a little chemistry on a smaller, related molecule. That’s a different result from having weak activity on the actual target. Still, it meant the search hadn’t come back entirely empty-handed.

An enzyme is a protein. Changing its building blocks can change which molecules it works on and how well it works. In directed evolution, researchers make variants, test their performance, and use promising variants as starting points for further changes. The tests give that development a direction; they don’t have to understand every structural detail in advance.

Here, that approach led to an enzyme useful for the sitagliptin reaction. We don’t have the structural explanation, but the development sequence is clear: a slight related ability became the starting point for engineering the ability they actually needed.

**Nell Arden**

Mira, sitagliptin already had a route being used in manufacture. What made it worth pursuing a different one?

**Mira Calder**

Because a working reaction can still leave a lot of work for the rest of the process.

The existing route used hydrogen gas under pressure and a rhodium catalyst—a metal that helps the reaction happen. That required specialized manufacturing equipment. So there was a practical incentive to find chemistry that didn’t need that high-pressure operation.

The other demand came from molecular handedness. Molecules can have mirror-image forms, rather like your left and right hands: the same parts, but arranged differently in space. For sitagliptin, the process needed to deliver one particular handed form.

The earlier reaction favoured the required form, but not sufficiently. The EPA account describes a crystallization step needed because of that shortfall. In other words, the reaction made a distinction imperfectly, and a later separation had to finish the job.

That’s where better reaction selectivity becomes interesting to a process chemist. It can change the work needed afterward, rather than simply make the reaction look better on paper. EPA reports that the enzymatic process eliminated both the high-pressure hydrogenation and that particular handedness-related purification step. That doesn’t mean all purification disappeared. But removing those specific operations is a concrete manufacturing advantage—and a substantial reason to reconsider a route that already worked.

**Nell Arden**

Leena, what changes for manufacturing when a route no longer needs that high-pressure hydrogen step?

**Leena Orrell**

It changes which equipment can do the job. A vessel isn’t suitable just because it’s large enough: it must also be designed for the pressure and the gas being handled. Hydrogenation requires equipment for containing and controlling pressurised hydrogen, as well as bringing that gas into contact with the reacting material.

Removing that operation takes away a specific demand on the plant. Imagine two otherwise useful reactors, but only one equipped for that hydrogenation. A route that no longer needs it potentially gives you more freedom to assign production. That can matter even if the reaction itself isn’t faster.

Here, EPA reports improved productivity using existing equipment. That’s a useful practical outcome, and I’d treat it as a result for the changed process—not simply a performance score for the enzyme.

The engineering question I’d then ask is how much acceptable product you recover for the equipment time you occupy. The reaction occupies only part of that time. Recovering the product and getting equipment ready for another batch also count. So a chemical change can improve manufacturing by changing what the equipment has to do, not only how quickly molecules react.

**Nell Arden**

Tomas, beyond getting the reaction to happen, what did the evolved enzyme achieve in choosing the molecule’s handedness?

**Tomas Venn**

It made the required handed form with very high selectivity. The EPA account reports no detectable formation of the unwanted mirror-image form, called the S-enantiomer.

That’s a separate achievement from activity. Activity tells us the enzyme can carry out the reaction on the target starting material. Selectivity tells us which handed product it makes. In principle, a catalyst could convert plenty of starting material but produce a mixture of both hands. More reaction wouldn’t necessarily solve that problem.

There’s a subtlety in the development story, too: the starting enzyme already had a handedness preference when working on the smaller, related molecule. That didn’t establish how it would perform on the actual target. The useful endpoint was an enzyme that could work on that target and deliver the required hand selectively.

“No detectable” means the unwanted form wasn’t found by the test—not proof that absolutely none existed, or that every other impurity was absent.

Here, EPA reports removal of that specific purification step: the reaction no longer left that sorting job to a later operation.

**Nell Arden**

Mira, how did the chemistry itself change between the two routes, beyond the choice of catalyst?

**Mira Calder**

The two reactions approached the same part of sitagliptin from different starting points. In the earlier route, the nitrogen needed at that position was already present in an intermediate called an enamine. The rhodium catalyst helped it react with hydrogen gas, producing the amine with a preference for the required handedness.

The enzyme route started instead from a ketone, which has oxygen at the corresponding position. A transaminase transfers an amino group—a nitrogen-containing group—from another molecule. That lets it turn the ketone into the required amine, while selecting the handedness. So the nitrogen arrives through a different chemical operation.

You don’t need to remember those names. The important distinction is that this wasn’t the same starting material undergoing the same reaction, with an enzyme standing in for rhodium. The immediate starting material changed, and so did the transformation used to reach the product.

For route selection, that takes my attention one step back. What do we have to prepare to feed the key reaction? And then one step forward: what work does its product still need? A very attractive catalyst can lose its advantage if supplying its starting material is difficult. Catalyst development and route development have to meet; neither can make the manufacturing choice on its own.

**Nell Arden**

Leena, what does recovering usable product involve that a successful reaction hasn’t yet accomplished?

**Leena Orrell**

At the end of a reaction, the desired molecule is generally still part of a mixture. Showing that it’s there—even in large amounts—isn’t the same as collecting usable active ingredient.

You have to separate it from material you don’t want and recover it in a condition that meets the ingredient’s quality requirements. The method depends on the route, and those operations can leave some of your product behind.

As a hypothetical example, suppose you collect a product as a solid, but some remains dissolved in the liquid you remove. The reaction made that material perfectly well. You simply haven’t recovered it. Trying to collect more might also bring along more unwanted material, so recovery isn’t just a matter of gathering every last molecule.

That’s why I’d follow the material beyond the reaction vessel. How much product formed, and how much acceptable ingredient was actually collected? Those are different measurements. A reaction test can answer the first without answering the second.

For this sitagliptin comparison, the reported removal of a separation step matters because recovery operations themselves take resources and can lose product. But to assess the whole process, I’d want the usable output after the remaining operations—not just the promising result inside the reactor.

**Nell Arden**

How far did the sitagliptin enzyme process get beyond a promising reaction, Leena?

**Leena Orrell**

It reached pilot-scale development and, later, company-reported use in manufacture. So the history doesn’t stop at a promising reaction.

The EPA’s 2010 account says Merck scaled the process to pilot scale during 2009. Pilot work is an intermediate stage where developers can examine how chemistry and practical operations work together at a larger scale. In that account, commercialization was still described as a plan.

Then, in September 2021, Codexis reported that its enzyme was being used in sitagliptin manufacture. It described a supply relationship with Merck dating from 2012 and announced an amended license-and-supply agreement running through the end of 2026.

That later report matters: it takes us beyond an intention to manufacture to a stated manufacturing use. But the agreement’s end date isn’t evidence that production or supply has continued uninterrupted up to today.

From an engineering perspective, those milestones answer a different question from the recovery question we’ve just discussed. They tell us the development progressed beyond the laboratory and was reported in manufacturing use. They don’t give us the batch records needed to judge how consistently it delivered acceptable product. We can acknowledge that substantial progress without treating a commercial announcement as a complete performance assessment.

**Nell Arden**

Tomas, how does aiming for manufacture change what you would test for when evolving an enzyme?

**Tomas Venn**

I’d test whether it keeps working in the mixture the process needs—not just whether it produces a reaction under convenient laboratory conditions. We don’t have the individual tests used in this sitagliptin programme, but we can explain that principle.

Suppose, hypothetically, one enzyme starts quickly but soon loses activity. Another starts more slowly and keeps going. A brief test might favour the first; measuring product over the intended reaction time might favour the second. You’re selecting for a different ability: sustained performance, rather than a promising burst.

The surrounding mixture matters too. If the proposed process needs a more concentrated starting material, testing only dilute mixtures won’t tell you whether the enzyme can do that job. Manufacturing requirements have to enter the development tests, rather than arrive as a surprise at scale-up. And you’d still check that the enzyme delivers the required handedness under those conditions.

You don’t necessarily impose every demand at the beginning. A small sign of activity can be enough to get development started—as the smaller analogue was here. But as development proceeds, the tests must become more demanding in the ways that matter to the process. Directed evolution follows what you select for; choosing the test is part of designing the catalyst.

**Nell Arden**

Mira, what does choosing those tests imply for how catalyst development and route development should work together?

**Mira Calder**

They need to develop together. The process tells you what to ask of the catalyst, but the catalyst’s capabilities can also tell you to revise the proposed process.

Take Tomas’s example of concentration. Suppose, hypothetically, an enzyme works beautifully only in a very dilute mixture. From the reaction’s point of view, that might be a success. From the route’s point of view, we now have much more liquid to handle for the same amount of product—and we still have to recover that product.

So I wouldn’t simply tell the enzyme developers, “Make it work at a higher concentration.” I’d want us to establish whether that change actually helps the subsequent operations. Perhaps it does, and it becomes an important development target. Perhaps the more dilute process is manageable, and another limitation deserves the effort instead.

That means testing promising catalysts in a small version of the proposed sequence, including product recovery, before declaring one the winner. You learn what the reaction leaves for the next operation to do.

The useful collaboration isn’t just agreeing on a demanding test. It’s agreeing on why that demand matters—and being willing to change either the catalyst or the route when the results come back.

**Tomas Venn**

That also gives us a reason to stop improving a particular enzyme property. In Mira’s hypothetical example, if working at higher concentration no longer helps the surrounding operations, it may be time to select for something else.

The judgement is deciding which improvement is worth pursuing—and when the enzyme is good enough for the job.

**Mira Calder**

That makes “good enough” a judgement about where to put the next experiment. If that enzyme property is no longer holding the process back, I’d look at what is. The useful next improvement might come from recovering the product more effectively, rather than making an already capable catalyst still better.

**Nell Arden**

So success means a useful process, not just a successful reaction. Here, demanding operations were removed—not every manufacturing question settled. Thank you, all three, and thanks for listening to Still Curious.
